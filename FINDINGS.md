# Can Texas Beat California? A Split Decision.

> Synopsis of the analysis. Every figure traces to the datasets in `data/processed/`
> and the tables/charts in `outputs/`. Dollars are inflation-adjusted where compared
> across years. Sources: BEA, Census (PEP/ACS), IRS SOI, SEC EDGAR, EIA/EIA-861,
> BLS QCEW, Zillow — 2015→2025.

For a decade the story sold itself: Americans and their companies were fleeing
high-tax, high-cost California for booming, business-friendly Texas. The data says
that story is **true** — and that it misses the bigger one.

**What moves, moves to Texas.** From 2015–2024, California lost a net **2.16 million
residents** to other states; Texas gained **1.33 million**. Follow the money and it's
sharper: tax returns show roughly **$98 billion of adjusted gross income**
(inflation-adjusted) drained from California's interstate ledger while Texas's *gained*
**$42 billion** — about **$19 billion and 340,000 people of it flowing California →
Texas directly**, peaking in 2021. The marquee names are real and SEC-verified: Tesla,
Oracle, HPE, Chevron, Charles Schwab, CBRE all now file from Texas addresses.

**What stays, stays in California — and it's the valuable part.** California's economy
is still **$3.39 trillion** to Texas's **$2.28T**. Even Texas's faster decade of growth
(**+39% vs +33%** real) hasn't dented that gap. Californians produce **~18% more per
person**, attract **twice the private capital** ($121B vs $61B in Form D filings), and
in the one sector that matters most right now — Information — the average California job
pays **$329,000 to Texas's $138,000**, a gap that has *widened* from 1.8× to 2.4× as the
AI boom concentrates the best-paid work on the coast.

**But Texas isn't free.** The cheap sticker hides the catch. Texas's effective
property-tax rate is nearly **double** California's — enough that, *as a share of
income*, the two states' property-tax burdens are nearly identical. Austin, the poster
child of the boom, posted the biggest home-price surge of any metro we measured
(**+73%**) and then the steepest crash (**−26%**). And when Winter Storm Uri hit in
2021, the average Texan lost **~19.6 hours** of power — **3.6× California** — exposing
the tail risk of an isolated grid (even if, on a normal day, Texas keeps the lights on
just as well).

**The open question.** Here's the twist the headlines miss: the great migration
**peaked in 2021 and has been cooling ever since** — California's net income loss has
already shrunk by more than half. Just as Texas's flow slows, the largest concentration
of capital and talent in a generation, frontier AI, is landing almost entirely in
California. Texas is winning the people. California is winning the highest-value industry
of the next decade. **The unresolved bet: do the people and paychecks eventually drag the
output with them — or does California's capital-and-AI moat hold long enough to make the
exodus a footnote?**
